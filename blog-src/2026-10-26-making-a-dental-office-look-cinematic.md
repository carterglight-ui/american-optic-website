There's something so frustrating about trying to make a dental office look cinematic. Don't get me wrong at all. Dental practices are absolutely excellent at being dental practices. They're clean, they're bright, they're well organized and they're all designed to make you feel at ease.

The problem is that almost none of those attributes really translate into anything particularly cinematic. White walls, white ceilings, overhead lighting, reflective surfaces. Rooms barely large enough to fit a dentist, a patient and a camera (let alone a proper lighting rig). And that's before you even get into the real problem: it's a running business, not a film set.

I've spent a fair bit of time doing video for dental practices all over Jacksonville, FL and one thing I've learned is that the camera itself is pretty much the easy bit. It's all the stuff going on around it.

## The Problem With Lighting a Room That's Already Bright

One of the biggest myths about professional video production is that more light equals a better image. In reality the first thing I often want to do when walking into a room is to switch off half the lights. Dental offices generally have very even overhead lighting. That's ideal for clinical work really, but not necessarily so for creating any sense of depth, contrast or separation between the subject and the background. When everything is equally bright everything starts to look flat.

For a contemporary setup imagine filming a dentist talking directly to the camera in a small treatment room. The room has bright overhead lighting, a big dental chair, cabinetry on pretty much every wall and possibly a window which is either absolutely fantastic or completely killing the exposure. You could technically just put a camera on a tripod, hit record and call it a day. But there's a difference between recording someone talking and actually producing an image.

My normal approach is to work out what lighting is already in place, what we can control and what lighting we can actually bring into the space. Sometimes that's as simple as using a single soft key light and letting the ambient room lighting take care of the rest. Other times it's about repositioning the subject, managing the window light or finding a camera angle that gives you more depth. It's not necessarily down to having the biggest lighting package. It's about working out what the room requires.

## The Real Enemy Is Usually Time

Lighting is one issue. Scheduling is another. When you're doing a commercial production you can quite often spend an entire day just on one scene. When you're making marketing videos for an active dental practice you'll probably have 15 or 20 minutes before the room's needed again.

The dentist may be finishing off a treatment. The front desk will be on the phone. Patients will be coming in. Someone's going to need the room you're setting up in. You're trying to get the lighting right, frame the shot, check your audio and make sure the person on camera is comfortable. If you're doing the filming, directing, lighting and audio yourself then every minute counts.

This is where preparation is just as important as creativity. Being a good videographer isn't just about knowing how to operate a camera. It's about being able to make decisions quickly and still produce a high quality piece of work. Sometimes you get the perfect shot. Sometimes you get the very best possible shot given the circumstances. And that's part of the job really knowing the difference.

## Making Something Ordinary Feel Interesting

One of the things I love most about commercial film is the challenge of making a fairly ordinary environment visually interesting. A dental office isn't a dramatic location. There's no need for beautiful scenery, no need for interesting architecture and no need for anything particularly elaborate in the way of production design. You've got a chair, some instruments, a dentist and a room.

But that doesn't mean the footage has to be boring. Camera placement, focal length, lighting direction, movement and composition can all have a massive effect on how a space feels.

A longer lens can help to clean up a cluttered background. A well placed light can provide separation between the subject and the room. A close up of a dentist preparing his instruments can be a very interesting piece of B-roll in its own right if you frame it correctly. These are minor decisions but they all add up. They're the difference between footage that just records a business and footage that actually goes some way to establishing the business' identity.

## Why I Think Businesses Should Care About Production Quality

Not every business requires a huge commercial production. In fact, most don't. But I do think there's value in producing content that appears to be intentional. Whether it's a dental practice, an auto dealership, a restaurant or a construction company the way a business presents itself visually makes a huge difference to how people see it.

Professional video production doesn't necessarily mean making everything look expensive. It's about making the business look like itself, at its very best.

For dental practices that's probably about creating a clinical environment that feels welcoming rather than intimidating. It could be about helping a nervous patient feel at ease when they're giving a testimonial. It could be about producing a short ad that conveys a sense of professionalism without being at all corporate. Those are creative problems rather than just technical ones. And they're the kind of problems I like to solve.

## What I'd Do Differently Next Time

Something I've come to realize is that every production teaches you something even if it does seem to be a bit of a repeat. You could film in the same building, with the same gear, with the same general aims and still the circumstances would be entirely different. Different people. Different rooms. Different lighting. Different time constraints.

The aim is to keep perfecting the process. Spend less time fixing things. Make better decisions earlier on. Prevent the problems before they occur. And hopefully on occasion you walk away with a shot that's better than the one you originally envisaged.

That's one of the reasons I've been keen to start recording these experiences. Not every shoot is glamorous. Most aren't. There's a surprisingly large amount of creative problem solving that goes into making even a 30 second video. And I think that's worth talking about.
